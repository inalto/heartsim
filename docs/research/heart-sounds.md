# Engine events can drive heart sounds

Heart sounds can be added to heartsim as a deterministic, parametric phonocardiogram (PCG) synthesised from the same `TimingEngine` events that already drive the ECG strip and the 3-D colour map, and this is the right route on all three counts the question raises: physiology, licensing and synchronisation. The literature ties every sound to an electrical landmark the engine already emits: S1 starts about 40 ms after QRS onset, A2 falls at QRS onset plus a rate-dependent interval (about 546 − 2.1 × heart rate ms in men), S4 follows the P wave, S3 follows A2 by 120–180 ms, S1 loudness tracks the PR interval and S2 splitting tracks the order of ventricular activation. Shippable recordings are too thin to build on: plain CC-BY/CC0 real recordings exist only for normal sounds, an innocent murmur, a few paediatric lesions and three regurgitant lesions, S3/S4/AF exist only as manikin output, and nothing shippable was found for mitral stenosis, PDA, ASD, pericardial rub, HOCM or prosthetic valves. The well-known PhysioNet sets are under ODC-By 1.0, which is not on the project's allow-list and needs an explicit decision. Synthesis also gives the simulator something recordings cannot: sounds that follow an arbitrary rhythm beat by beat (cannon sounds in complete block, paradoxical splitting in LBBB, a louder ejection murmur after a PVC). The recommended design is a pure, wasm-clean PCG synthesiser next to the ECG synthesiser, written into a ring buffer that is both drawn under the ECG and streamed to the audio device with a fixed, compensated delay; the teaching layer should follow the evidence, which favours high-volume repetition of a small sound set, a synchronised trace, and self-testing with feedback. Many numeric details rest on secondary sources or on search extracts of paywalled papers, and the dB values, per-site gains and murmur envelope fractions are design choices, not published measurements; these are marked throughout.

Three labels are used in this report. "Sources state" covers what a cited page or the repository says. "Inference" marks reasoning by the researchers or by me. "Unverified" marks items that were seen only as search extracts, recalled from memory, or not confirmed by running code.

## Every heart sound hangs on an electrical landmark the engine already emits

### Timing against the ECG

Sources state that S1 has two components, mitral (M1) then tricuspid (T1), normally 20–30 ms apart, with M1 produced by the abrupt rise of left-ventricular pressure at the start of isovolumic contraction ([Felner, Clinical Methods ch. 22](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK333/)). The interval from QRS onset to S1 is about **40 ms** at a PR of 180–190 ms, and it shortens and the sound softens as the PR lengthens ([Leech et al. 1980](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC482253), seen as an extract); a 2025 synchronised ECG/PCG study measured a mean of 40.45 ms against a reference near 45 ms ([PubMed 40648475](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40648475/), extract). Signal-processing work uses a coarser convention, treating S1 as starting at the R peak with a 100 ms tolerance ([Springer et al. 2016](https://moody-challenge.physionet.org/2022/refs/LR_HSMM_Segmentation_IEEE_Springer_Tarassenko_Clifford.pdf)), which is a labelling approximation and not a physiological value.

The second sound is the better-quantified one. Weissler's regressions put the interval from QRS onset to aortic closure at **QS2 = 546 − 2.1 × HR ms in men and 549 − 2.0 × HR ms in women** ([Boudoulas et al. 1979](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/pdf/10.1161/01.cir.60.1.164), extract), and the slopes are confirmed in a paper read in full ([Orter et al. 2022](https://elib.dlr.de/190261/1/EPH-107-213.pdf)). That gives 420 ms at 60 bpm, 388 ms at 75 bpm and 336 ms at 100 bpm. S2 onset follows the end of the T wave by a median of only **5 ms** in 99 normal recordings, although the two are not interchangeable beat by beat (regression slope 0.6) ([Sbrollini et al. 2017](https://cinc.org/archives/2017/pdf/085-076.pdf)). P2 follows A2 because the pressure "hangout" is at most 5 ms on the left and 30–120 ms on the right ([Felner, ch. 23](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK341/)).

S3 falls **120–160 ms after A2** at the end of rapid filling and does not depend on atrial activity ([Felner, ch. 23](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK341/)); other sources widen the window to 100–240 ms ([Nemani & Pechetty, Part 2](https://ijcdw.org/additional-heart-sounds-part-2-clicks-opening-snap-and-more/)). S4 is produced by atrial contraction, sits just before S1, needs an effective atrial systole and is therefore absent in atrial fibrillation ([Williams, Clinical Methods ch. 25](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK344/); [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)). No opened source gave a P-onset-to-S4 interval; the working value of roughly 100–140 ms after P onset is derived, not cited.

Published frequency bands disagree widely across sensors and definitions (S1 quoted anywhere from 25–45 Hz to 50–150 Hz), so any single band is indicative only.

### Loudness and splitting carry the rhythm information

Sources state that S1 is loudest at a PR of **80–120 ms**, falls as the PR lengthens and is soft or absent beyond about 200 ms, because the leaflets have already drifted closed ([Felner, ch. 22](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK333/)). Anything that varies the atrioventricular relationship beat to beat varies S1: Wenckebach, AV dissociation in complete block or ventricular tachycardia, and atrial fibrillation ([Felner, ch. 22](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK333/)); the MSD Manual lists beat-to-beat variation of S1 among the signs of complete block ([MSD Manual, AV block](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/specific-cardiac-arrhythmias/atrioventricular-block)). One finding contradicts common teaching: the short PR of WPW does not reliably give a loud S1, because ventricular contraction starts later than the delta wave suggests ([Felner, ch. 22](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK333/)).

Splitting of S2 follows ventricular activation order. Delayed right-ventricular activation (RBBB, LV ectopic or LV-paced beats) delays P2 and gives a persistent split that still widens on inspiration; an atrial septal defect gives a wide split that changes by less than 20 ms; delayed left-ventricular activation or ejection (LBBB, RV pacing, outflow obstruction) reverses the order, so the split is widest in expiration ([Felner, ch. 23](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK341/); [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)). The only directly sourced magnitude for a paced rhythm is a single case: RV pacing produced a paradoxical split of 100 ms in expiration and 70 ms in inspiration ([Eur Heart J Case Rep 2023](https://academic.oup.com/ehjcr/article/7/5/ytad233/7150401)). Animal pacing data show the split moving from 47 ms with LV pre-excitation to 23 ms with RV pre-excitation ([Luo et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7785055/)). The ear resolves a split from about 20 ms and hears it reliably from 40 ms ([Felner, ch. 23](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK341/); [Luo et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7785055/)).

Inference: a single rule reproduces all of this. Schedule M1 and A2 from left-ventricular activation and T1 and P2 from right-ventricular activation, add a respiratory term to the A2–P2 base interval, and compute S1 gain from the interval between the last atrial activation and QRS onset. Felner notes that LBBB does not split S1, so M1 should be delayed by less than the full electrical delay in LBBB. The decibel values of the S1 gain curve, the treatment of AF (gain from the preceding RR), silence in VF and asystole, and dropping S2 on very early PVCs are standard clinical reasoning but had no citable source in the notes.

### Murmurs are envelopes between the same anchors

Sources state that ejection murmurs start after a silent gap following S1, end before S2 and peak progressively later as stenosis worsens; regurgitant systolic murmurs run as a plateau from S1 to or through A2; aortic regurgitation is a decrescendo from A2; mitral stenosis is an opening snap followed by a low rumble with presystolic accentuation ([Alpert, Clinical Methods ch. 26](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK345/); [Crawley, ch. 27](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK346/)). Ejection murmurs grow louder on the beat after a PVC, whereas the mitral regurgitation murmur does not change comparably ([Alpert, ch. 26](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK345/)). The MSD Manual states that the presystolic accentuation of mitral stenosis is lost in atrial fibrillation ([MSD Manual, mitral stenosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/mitral-stenosis)); an older report that it can persist in short cycles was not retrieved and is unverified. No opened source gives millisecond values for murmur onset and end, nor the fraction of systole at which the aortic stenosis murmur peaks per severity grade, so those are parameter choices.

Two caveats matter. The HLS-CMDS authors license their recordings, but the sounds come from a commercial manikin's built-in library, and whether the vendor holds rights in the underlying sounds is not addressed on the pages read ([Mendeley record](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/8972jxbpmp/3)); this needs a short legal check before it is the project's only S3/S4 sample source. Inference: the shippable real recordings are a heterogeneous handful (1980s paediatric tape excerpts, digital-stethoscope clips, manikin output) with no coverage of mitral stenosis, PDA, ASD, rub, HOCM or prosthetic valves and no recording in which the rhythm is under the simulator's control. They are valuable as labelled "real example" clips in a reference library and for tuning by ear, and SensSmartTech is the set to validate QRS-to-S1 and T-end-to-S2 timing against, across 58–173 bpm. They cannot be the sound engine. Attribution is straightforward: one table per shipped file with author, source DOI or URL, licence and changes made satisfies CC BY, and conversion or trimming counts as a change to be indicated.

## Repetition, a synchronised trace and self-testing are what the training evidence supports

Sources state that baseline skill is poor at every level. Residents recognised **20%** of 12 recorded events, no better than students ([Mangione & Nieman, JAMA 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286830/)), and in 860 participants scores did not improve from the third year of medical school through to faculty ([Vukanovic-Criley et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.166.6.610)). The strongest remedy on record is repetition: 500 listens to each of four basic murmurs lifted second-year students from about 14–21% to **85–86%**, with an unmonitored group doing as well as a monitored one and controls unchanged ([Barrett et al., Chest 2004](https://doi.org/10.1378/chest.126.2.470)). Auscultation behaves partly as a technical skill acquired by exposure.

A synchronised visual trace adds a modest, real gain. In a randomised trial of 135 first-year students, training with a phonocardiogram video with a sweeping cursor raised audio-only test scores from 61% to 70% on key features (d = 0.45) and from 59% to 68% on diagnosis (d = 0.40) ([Watsjold et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40037-020-00646-5)). That module used a chest diagram with four listening sites, feature tick-boxes, a diagnosis choice and immediate feedback, told learners to use headphones and gated entry with a 200 Hz tone check. A viewing aid helps experienced listeners more for gallops: accuracy for S3 rose 8–18% and for S4 15–32%, against 2% and 11% in first-year students ([Michaels et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/clc.20586)). Simulation training in general has a pooled effect of 0.87 on skills with high heterogeneity ([McKinney et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-012-2198-y)), mastery learning with a pass mark transfers to real patients ([Butter et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-010-1309-x)), and high cognitive load during simulation cut the odds of recognising a trained murmur to 0.27 ([Fraser et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2012.04355.x)). Learners persistently confuse ejection with regurgitant murmurs and over-call innocent murmurs ([Finley et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2923.1998.00210.x); [Germanakis et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.12062)).

Inference, for what to teach and how. Start with a small core, S1 and S2 identification, split S2, S3, S4 and the four or five classic murmurs, and widen later to the 14-event set used in published tests ([March et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.4065/80.11.1443)). Build the core loop around short loopable clips with a listen counter, since unsupervised repetition works. Show the PCG under the ECG with a moving cursor, and make it hideable, because the trials tested audio-only. Add contrast pairs (innocent versus aortic stenosis, ejection versus regurgitant) and a quiz that asks for features before diagnosis. Keep the listening screen sparse. heartsim's distinctive content is the link the evidence base has not tested: hearing S1 fade as the PR lengthens on the strip, or the split reverse when LBBB appears.

The evidence stops short in places. No controlled study was found on slowed playback, on pitch-shifting gallops for small speakers, or on headphones versus speakers. Half-speed replay is recommended by an expert review ([Tavel 2006](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.105.591149)), and one paper reports that 80% of 40 students judged clinical information preserved after upward pitch shifting for phone playback ([Personal and Ubiquitous Computing 2024](https://investigadores.anid.cl//en/public_search/work?id=866963), abstract only). Both are aids without proven learning effect and should be labelled as altered sound.

## Synthesis fits the codebase; the present beep path cannot carry heart sounds

### What the literature offers for synthesis

Sources state three implementable families. The chirp model of Xu, Durand and Pibarot treats A2 and P2 as short chirps of falling frequency; the trajectory quoted for simulation is **A2 from 250 Hz to 53 Hz and P2 from 200 Hz to 50 Hz over 60 ms** ([Djebbari & Bereksi-Reguig 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3706289/)). Gaussian-windowed sinusoid atoms phase-locked to the ECG R peak underlie the Almasi dynamical model ([PMC3390312](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3390312), search-snippet level) and the 2026 SynPCG generator, which also models murmurs as band-limited noise under a cycle-anchored envelope ([Nonaka & Seita 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlresearch/v297/main/assets/nonaka26a/nonaka26a.pdf)). A fetal simulator uses attack-plus-exponential-decay sinusoids and an AR(1) noise floor ([Zhou et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16792v1)). Learned generators exist but, in the one clinician listening study, produced heart-like clips with weak abnormality cues ([Bao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.02448)). No reusable open implementation was found: the SynPCG and fetal-simulator repositories carry no licence ([SynPCG](https://github.com/seitalab/SynPCG); [FH-Simulator](https://github.com/ytzhou123/FH-Simulator)), so the equations must be re-implemented from the papers. Where documented, commercial trainers play recordings of real patients ([Kyoto Kagaku listing](https://www.medicalexpo.com/prod/kyoto-kagaku/product-69057-830784.html)), a vendor claim.

Three limits apply. No listener-realism study exists for any parametric model. No peer-reviewed table gives centre frequency and bandwidth per lesion; the figures found (general murmur energy 25–400 Hz, aortic stenosis peak above 300 Hz) come from reviews and patents and conflict. No measured table gives per-site attenuation of each component, so the four-area mix must be a designed gain table backed by the qualitative statements (S1 louder at the apex, A2 dominant at the aortic area, P2 heard only at the pulmonic area). The exact amplitude and phase constants of the Xu model were recalled from memory and are unverified; SynPCG's parameter table prints no units.

### What the repository offers

Sources (the repository) state that `TimingEngine` emits a chronological stream of `Event { at, region, kind, origin, chamber }` with exact `f64` millisecond timestamps (`crates/heartsim-sim/src/engine.rs:138-148`), covering atrial activation, each bundle branch as conducted or blocked, ventricular activation, pacing channel and circuit rhythms. It publishes no per-beat record, no QRS duration and no mechanical quantity; QRS morphology is decided inside the ECG synthesiser (`crates/heartsim-ecg/src/synth.rs`), and respiration exists only as a sinus-rate modulation without a published phase (`crates/heartsim-sim/src/timing/params.rs:98-105`). The ECG path is a direct model for a PCG: events feed `GaussianSynth`, which is sampled into generic `LeadRing` buffers at 500 Hz, with randomness a pure function of event time (`crates/heartsim-app/src/app.rs:364-401`; `crates/heartsim-ecg/src/ring.rs`). `Tier0Scenario::record` renders a scenario offline and already feeds the rhythm quiz. Scenario files carry engine sections that each crate reads from the same RON text, and no crate denies unknown fields, so an optional `auscultation` section fits the format without a version bump.

The existing monitor beep is a 1 kHz tone played "now" once per frame; its own documentation says it is not sample-accurate (`crates/heartsim-app/src/monitor_beep.rs:12-13`), and it is compiled only for desktop behind the `audio` feature. The speed control spans **0.05× to 2×** (`crates/heartsim-ui/src/transport.rs:10`). kira 0.12.6 accepts a custom `Sound` whose `process()` runs on the audio thread, quantises scheduled starts to its 128-frame internal buffer (about 3 ms), and exposes no output-latency figure; on wasm it runs its mixer on the main thread through cpal's Web Audio host with 2048-frame buffers ([docs.rs/kira/0.12.6](https://docs.rs/kira/0.12.6); [docs.rs/cpal/0.18.2](https://docs.rs/cpal/0.18.2)). In the browser, the documented pattern is to schedule finished buffers on the `AudioContext` clock about 100 ms ahead ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/audio-scheduling)), and `baseLatency` plus `outputLatency` report the output delay ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioContext/outputLatency)). The 3-D view has no contraction or valve motion; valve meshes are render-only and flat-coloured, and glyph overlays and the skin-marker machinery of the human figure are available hooks (`crates/heartsim-app/src/heart_view.rs`).

### Why synchronisation needs a fixed delay

Inference: per-frame triggering delivers an event up to one 17 ms frame late with one frame of jitter, before device latency. That is tolerable for a beep and wrong for sounds whose spacing is the lesson; a split of 30–60 ms cannot be rendered that way at all. The broadcast standard puts detectability of audio-video offset at about 45 ms when sound leads and 125 ms when it lags ([ITU-R BT.1359-1](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bt/R-REC-BT.1359-1-199811-I!!PDF-E.pdf), as quoted in search results), so the rule is: never early, and late by a small constant. Two facts make that achievable. Every sound is causal in simulation time, since S1 follows the QRS event, A2 is predictable at the QRS from the preceding RR, S3 follows A2, S4 and presystolic murmurs follow the atrial event, and a diastolic murmur can simply run until the next ventricular event ends it. And the app already draws the past from ring buffers when scrubbing. A continuous PCG stream played a fixed delay behind the simulation therefore keeps intra-beat timing sample-exact regardless of frame jitter, and the residual constant offset can be removed by drawing the strip head and 3-D map the same delay behind. A threshold for simple transient stimuli, closer to "S1 against a moving trace" than lip-sync, was not sourced; the 20–30 ms target is a conservative goal.

## Recommended design: one pure PCG synthesiser, drawn and played from the same samples

Everything in this section is a design recommendation, built on the findings above.

**A pure synthesis layer.** Add a `pcg` module to `heartsim-ecg` (it can become its own crate between `sim` and `render` if it grows), with no audio dependency, compiled on wasm32. It has three parts. A beat assembler turns the event stream into per-beat records: P time, QRS onset, PR, preceding RR, origin, blocked branches, paced chamber, pre-excitation, atrial contraction present or not. The branch-block classification now private to `GaussianSynth` moves into a small shared function, so strip morphology and split direction can never disagree. An electromechanical model turns each record into scheduled components using the sourced rules: M1 at QRS + 40 ms modulated by PR, T1 20–30 ms later, A2 at QRS + QS2 from the preceding RR, P2 from the respiratory base interval plus the interventricular delay, S3 and S4 when the scenario enables them and physiology permits, S1 gain from the P-to-QRS interval. A synthesiser renders components as closed-form atoms: falling-frequency chirps for valve closures, low-frequency atoms for gallops, short high-frequency atoms for clicks and snaps, and seeded noise through fixed-coefficient biquads under cycle-anchored envelopes for murmurs. Noise and jitter derive from scenario seed and event time, as `event_noise` does for the ECG.

**A fixed internal rate of 4 kHz.** It gives 2 kHz of bandwidth, enough for the murmur energy the sources describe, four samples per 1 ms engine step, an integer ratio to 48 kHz, and under 1 MB for a 60 s ring. BMD-HS is recorded at the same rate ([BMD-HS](https://github.com/sani002/BMD-HS-Dataset)). The PCG goes into a `LeadRing` and is drawn under the ECG on the same time axis, with audio on or off, which also serves silent classrooms and hearing-impaired learners.

**Scenario format.** An optional `auscultation: (...)` section lists lesions with severity, S3, S4 and the S2-split mode, defaulting to normal; the 70 existing scenarios get rhythm-derived sounds with no authoring. Timeline changes and manoeuvres come later as a new `Action` variant, and `ExpectedFindings` gains optional auscultation fields for quizzes and golden tests. The engine should expose a respiratory phase so inspiratory splitting and sinus arrhythmia share one clock.

**Device sinks, write-only.** A sink trait hides the platform, as `BeepSink` does. On desktop, a custom kira `Sound` pops samples from a lock-free SPSC queue (`rtrb`, already in the dependency tree), resamples to the device rate and emits silence on underrun; the queue targets about 40 ms, and the beep moves onto the same `AudioManager`. On the web, the same chunks are handed to Web Audio as `AudioBuffer`s with `start(when)` and a look-ahead of about 100 ms, bypassing kira; the context is created or resumed from the learner's click on the sound switch, with a DOM-level listener as the conservative fallback. Nothing flows back from an audio clock into `SimClock`; drift is handled on the audio side by trimming or padding in silence. When sound is on, the strip head and 3-D map are drawn a constant delay behind (queue depth plus reported latency on the web, plus a user "audio offset" setting on desktop, where kira reports none).

**Speed, pause, scrub.** At 1× the played samples are the drawn samples. Below 1×, a second synthesiser instance fed the same events renders in wall time: each atom keeps its natural pitch and duration and only the intervals stretch, which is exactly the "hear the split in slow motion" use and needs no vocoder; the trace still shows the true signal. Above 1×, mute heart sounds and leave the beep. Pause fades out and clears the queue; while paused, "play from cursor" plays a slice of the PCG ring with a moving playhead; reload and backlog drops re-anchor.

**Listening sites and visuals.** Five clickable sites on the human figure (aortic, pulmonic, Erb's point, tricuspid, mitral) select a per-component gain vector from a RON table, with bell and diaphragm as two filter presets. Glyphs at the four valve landmarks pulse from the same component schedule, honouring reduced motion. The gain table and filter corners are designed values and should be documented as such.

**Teaching layer.** An auscultation tutorial opens with headphone advice and a two-tone sound check. An auscultation quiz copies `RhythmQuiz`: an offline `record()` returns ECG and PCG, the clip loops as a static buffer, the learner ticks features then picks a diagnosis from `confused_with` distractors, with the trace hidden until answered. A repetition trainer loops single findings with a per-sound listen counter. A "small speaker" mode that multiplies component frequencies is cheap in a parametric synthesiser and must be off by default and labelled.

**Recordings.** Ship a small reference library of CC0 and CC-BY clips (normal, Still's murmur, AS, VSD, MVP, AR, MR, TR) with an attribution table, presented as "real recording" beside the synthesised version. Nothing comes from `epicardio/`. Use the study-only sets for tuning by ear and SensSmartTech for timing validation.

## What could not be verified

Several classical primary papers (Weissler 1968, Leech 1980, Boudoulas 1979, Burggraf & Craige 1974) were seen only as search-engine extracts, so the QS2 intercepts, the 40 ms Q–S1 figure and the PR-to-loudness relation should be re-read in the PDFs before being cited in parameter files; Weissler's LVET and PEP intercepts were not confirmed at all and are omitted here. No source gave the P-to-S4 interval, S3 or S4 durations, decibel relations between PR and S1, split widths with mean and deviation for RBBB, LBBB or ASD, murmur onset and end in milliseconds, or per-site amplitude ratios. Auscultation in Mobitz I and II, flutter, VF and very early PVCs is reasoned, not cited. On licences, the CinC 2016 file listing was read only in part, the Michigan library's licence was seen through a search snippet, Freesound pages were not readable, and the Commons enumeration is incomplete. On engineering, nothing was built or run: kira with a custom `Sound` on wasm32, real output latencies per desktop backend, whether eframe's web runner executes app code inside the user-gesture task, and whether a kira clock can be slaved to simulation speed are all untested. The synthesis notes cite a web size budget that was not checked against the repository, and `docs/web.md` still says the app has no sound, which is stale.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the feature. Heart sounds turn out to be less a new subsystem than a second read-out of state the simulator already has: once a per-beat record exists, most of auscultation in rhythm disorders follows from three intervals (P to QRS, left versus right ventricular activation, preceding RR) and a respiratory phase. That is also where heartsim can teach something recorded-sound trainers cannot, because a recording cannot soften its S1 when the learner lengthens the PR. The licence survey points the same way from the opposite side: the allow-list leaves too few recordings to build on, so synthesis is the only route that covers every condition, and the few shippable clips become honest reference examples next to it.

The main risk is realism, not synchronisation. Timing can be made exact by construction and checked by golden tests against a CC-BY dataset with simultaneous ECG. Timbre, per-site balance and murmur character rest on designed parameters with no published benchmark, so the project should label them as models, keep the true trace visible, and put a clinician's ears on Phase 4 before the sounds are presented as what a stethoscope delivers.
