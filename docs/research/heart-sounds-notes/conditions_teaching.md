# Cardiac auscultation: condition catalogue and evidence on teaching with recorded/simulated sounds

Method note for the report writer. PubMed abstracts were retrieved verbatim through NCBI E-utilities (numbers from them are exact). MSD Manual (Professional) pages and one University of Washington page were read through an automated page-summariser, so wording is paraphrased and individual details deserve a spot-check before they are hard-coded. NCBI Bookshelf (Clinical Methods, StatPearls) and PMC pages were blocked by a browser check and could NOT be read; nothing below is taken from them. Abbreviations: LSB/RSB = left/right sternal border, ICS = intercostal space, LLD = left lateral decubitus, AS/AR/MS/MR/TR = valve lesions, HOCM = hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy.

## Part A — Which conditions and rhythms have characteristic auscultation findings, and what are they?

### Takeaway
Almost every scenario a rhythm/ECG simulator already models has an auscultatory correlate that can be generated from engine timing: S1 loudness tracks the PR interval and AV synchrony, S2 splitting tracks the order of ventricular activation (RBBB wide, LBBB/RV pacing paradoxical), gallops track filling/atrial kick (S4 disappears in AF), and murmur loudness tracks cycle length (post-ectopic beat, AF). Valve and congenital lesions add a fixed set of murmurs whose timing, shape, site and response to manoeuvres are well standardised, with quantified diagnostic accuracy available for AS, AR, HOCM, MR/VSD, right-sided murmurs and the S3.

### Cited Findings

#### General rules that apply to all scenarios
- Diaphragm for high-pitched sounds, bell with very light pressure for low-pitched sounds (firm pressure removes very low-pitched sounds). Listening sequence: apex in LLD, then lower LSB upward, then right upper sternal border downward; lean forward for aortic/pulmonic diastolic murmurs and rubs. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- S1 and clicks are high-pitched and short; S3, S4 and knocks are low-pitched, softer and longer; the mitral opening snap is the exception (very high-pitched, brief). Systolic murmurs graded 1–6, diastolic 1–4. Diastolic and continuous murmurs are always abnormal. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- S1 is loudest at the apex, lower-pitched and longer than S2, and just precedes the carotid upstroke; S2 is loudest at the base. — [UW Advanced Physical Diagnosis](https://depts.washington.edu/physdx/heart/tech.html)
- Manoeuvres (table): inspiration augments right-sided sounds and murmurs and reduces left-sided ones; Valsalva strain augments HOCM and MVP murmurs and reduces AS, MR; squatting augments AR, AS, MR, MS and reduces HOCM and MVP; isometric handgrip augments MR, AR, MS rumble and reduces AS, HOCM, MVP murmurs. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- Quantified manoeuvre accuracy (50 patients): right-sided murmurs louder with inspiration (sens 100%, spec 88%); HOCM louder with Valsalva (65%/96%) and squat-to-stand (95%/84%), softer with stand-to-squat (95%/85%), passive leg raise (85%/91%), handgrip (85%/75%); MR and VSD louder with handgrip (68%/92%) and transient arterial occlusion (78%/100%); AS has no single identifying manoeuvre (diagnosis by exclusion). — [Lembo et al., NEJM 1988, doi:10.1056/NEJM198806163182404](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM198806163182404)
- Chest-wall distribution is the most useful murmur feature: "apical-base" pattern → raised aortic velocity (LR 9.7), "broad apical" → significant MR (LR 6.8), "left lower sternal" → significant TR (LR 8.4); many classic findings are absent in patients with significant lesions (376 inpatients vs echo). — [McGee, Am J Med 2010, doi:10.1016/j.amjmed.2010.04.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2010.04.027)

#### Normal adult
- S1 then S2; S2 physiologically split (A2 before P2) audibly only on inspiration. A soft mid-systolic "innocent" flow murmur without obstruction may be heard (2nd ICS; common in children, pregnancy, anaemia; aortic sclerosis in the elderly). Venous hum (continuous, right supraclavicular) is normal in children. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)

#### Athlete / young adult
- S3 can be normal in children, sometimes persisting to about age 40, and in pregnancy; S4 is always abnormal. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- Athlete's heart: bradycardia, displaced/enlarged LV impulse, systolic ejection flow murmur at the left lower sternal border, S3 from rapid early filling; an S4 is almost always abnormal in an athlete. ECG: sinus bradycardia (rarely <40/min) with sinus arrhythmia, first-degree AV block, Mobitz I at rest that disappears with exercise, incomplete RBBB, voltage criteria, early repolarisation; abnormal: complete LBBB, pre-excitation, Mobitz II/third-degree block, T inversion, ≥2 PVCs. — [MSD Manual, Athlete's Heart](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/sports-and-the-heart/athlete-s-heart)

#### First-degree AV block
- S1 soft or absent, because the AV leaflets drift almost closed before ventricular systole. ECG: PR > 0.20 s, every P conducted. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation); [MSD Manual, AV Block](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/specific-cardiac-arrhythmias/atrioventricular-block)

#### Mobitz I and Mobitz II
- ECG: Mobitz I — progressive PR lengthening to a dropped QRS, shortest PR after the pause, pause shorter than two RR intervals; Mobitz II — constant PR with intermittently non-conducted P waves, pause equal to two RR intervals. The source gives no auscultatory description for either. — [MSD Manual, AV Block](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/specific-cardiac-arrhythmias/atrioventricular-block)

#### Complete (third-degree) heart block
- Signs of AV dissociation: cannon a waves in the neck, beat-to-beat variation in the loudness of S1, and fluctuating blood pressure. Escape rate 35–50/min (narrow QRS, above His bifurcation) or 20–40/min (wide QRS, below it). ECG: P rate > QRS rate, no P–QRS relationship. — [MSD Manual, AV Block](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/specific-cardiac-arrhythmias/atrioventricular-block)
- Complete heart block is also listed among causes of a mid-diastolic flow murmur across a non-stenotic AV valve. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)

#### Atrial fibrillation
- Irregularly irregular pulse, pulse deficit (apical rate exceeds radial rate after closely coupled beats), loss of jugular a waves. ECG: no P waves, f waves (best in V1), irregularly irregular RR. — [MSD Manual, Atrial Fibrillation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/specific-cardiac-arrhythmias/atrial-fibrillation)
- S4 is absent in AF (no atrial contraction). — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- In MS with AF the presystolic accentuation of the rumble is lost, and murmur intensity varies with cycle length (louder after long cycles). — [MSD Manual, Mitral Stenosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/mitral-stenosis)

#### Sinus tachycardia / bradycardia
- In tachycardia S3 and S4 merge into a summation gallop as diastole shortens; a friction rub may sound almost continuous. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- Sinus tachycardia is the most common sign of PE and tamponade (pooled sensitivity for tamponade 77%). — [MSD Manual, PE](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pulmonary-disorders/pulmonary-embolism-pe/pulmonary-embolism-pe); [Roy et al., JAMA 2007, doi:10.1001/jama.297.16.1810](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.297.16.1810)

#### Premature ventricular / atrial beats
- The beat after a PVC makes an AS murmur louder (greater filling during the compensatory pause); HOCM responds to the standard manoeuvres in the opposite direction to AS. — [MSD Manual, Aortic Stenosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/aortic-stenosis)

#### Ventricular tachycardia
- ECG: wide QRS (≥0.12 s), dissociated P waves, fusion and capture beats, precordial concordance, north-west axis. The MSD VT page gives no bedside signs; the signs of AV dissociation (cannon a waves, variable S1, variable BP) are documented on the AV-block page. — [MSD Manual, VT](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/specific-cardiac-arrhythmias/ventricular-tachycardia-vt); [MSD Manual, AV Block](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/specific-cardiac-arrhythmias/atrioventricular-block)

#### RBBB
- Wide splitting of S2 that still widens further with inspiration (delayed P2); same pattern in pulmonic stenosis. "Wide physiologic splitting of S2 in RBBB" was one of 14 key events in a teaching/testing set. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation); [March et al., Mayo Clin Proc 2005, doi:10.4065/80.11.1443](https://doi.org/10.4065/80.11.1443)

#### LBBB and ventricular (RV) pacing
- Paradoxical (reversed) splitting: aortic closure is late, so S2 splits on expiration and becomes single on inspiration; causes named are LBBB and AS. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- RV pacing: case report of prominent paradoxical S2 splitting with RV-pacing-induced heart failure that markedly improved after upgrade to CRT. — [Arakaki et al., Eur Heart J Case Rep 2023, doi:10.1093/ehjcr/ytad233](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjcr/ytad233)

#### WPW
- A tertiary source lists WPW with right-ventricular pre-excitation, RV pacing and LBBB as causes of paradoxical S2 splitting (low-grade source; not verified in a primary reference). — [Wikipedia, Split S2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_S2)
- Ventricular pre-excitation may be present on the ECG in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. — [MSD Manual, HCM](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/cardiomyopathies/hypertrophic-cardiomyopathy)

#### Acute MI and its mechanical complications
- S4 is almost always present in active ischaemia or soon after MI. Papillary muscle dysfunction gives a mid-to-late systolic murmur; S1 remains distinct in MR due to papillary muscle dysfunction. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- Functional papillary insufficiency: apical late-systolic murmur, usually transient. Papillary muscle rupture: sudden loud apical holosystolic murmur and thrill with pulmonary oedema (severe MR can occasionally be silent). Ventricular septal rupture: sudden loud systolic murmur with thrill medial to the apex along the LSB at the 3rd–4th ICS, with hypotension. Free-wall rupture: no murmur, sudden loss of pressure with sinus rhythm briefly persisting, tamponade. Post-MI pericarditis: rub 24–96 h after onset with diffuse ST elevation. Aneurysm: persistent ST elevation. — [MSD Manual, Complications of ACS](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/coronary-artery-disease/complications-of-acute-coronary-syndromes)

#### Heart failure
- S3 in early diastole (rapid filling of a dilated, non-compliant ventricle): LV S3 best at the apex in LLD on expiration, RV S3 on inspiration supine; heard best or only with the bell. S3 ± S4 typifies systolic dysfunction, S4 without S3 diastolic dysfunction; summation gallop with tachycardia. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- Also: accentuated P2, pansystolic apical MR murmur with a dilated LV, pulsus alternans, displaced sustained apex. ECG not diagnostic (prior MI, LVH, LBBB, rapid AF raise suspicion). — [MSD Manual, Heart Failure](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/heart-failure/heart-failure-hf)
- In dyspnoeic ED patients an S3 gallop has LR+ 11 (95% CI 4.9–25) for heart failure; AF on ECG LR+ 3.8. — [Wang et al., JAMA 2005, doi:10.1001/jama.294.15.1944](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.294.15.1944)
- Phonocardiographic S3: sensitivity 41%/52%/32% and specificity 92%/87%/92% for raised LVEDP / reduced LVEF / raised BNP; S4: sensitivity 46%/43%/40%, specificity 80%/72%/78% (n = 90). — [Marcus et al., JAMA 2005, doi:10.1001/jama.293.18.2238](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.293.18.2238)
- Alternation of heart sounds, murmurs and the S3 in congestive failure was included as a teaching event. — [March et al., Mayo Clin Proc 2005, doi:10.4065/80.11.1443](https://doi.org/10.4065/80.11.1443)

#### LVH / hypertension
- S4 arises in late diastole from atrial contraction into a poorly compliant ventricle; bell, similar to S3; LV S4 decreases with inspiration. Hypertension is listed as a cause of aortic-root dilation and AR. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)

#### Pulmonary hypertension
- RV heave, widely split S2 with accentuated P2, pulmonary ejection click (early systolic, near S1), RV S3, TR murmur. ECG: right axis deviation, R > S in V1, peaked P in II. — [MSD Manual, Pulmonary Hypertension](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pulmonary-disorders/pulmonary-hypertension/pulmonary-hypertension); [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- Graham Steell murmur: soft decrescendo diastolic murmur along the LSB from pulmonary regurgitation in severe pulmonary hypertension. — [MSD Manual, Mitral Stenosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/mitral-stenosis)

#### Pulmonary embolism
- Tachycardia and tachypnoea are the commonest signs; a loud P2 is possible but uncommon in acute PE (pulmonary pressure rises only modestly); RV S3 gallop, RV heave and distended neck veins with RV failure. ECG: sinus tachycardia and non-specific ST-T changes most often; S1Q3T3 or new RBBB in about 5% (more in massive PE); right axis, P pulmonale, T inversion V1–V4. — [MSD Manual, Pulmonary Embolism](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pulmonary-disorders/pulmonary-embolism-pe/pulmonary-embolism-pe)

#### Aortic stenosis
- Harsh/grating crescendo-decrescendo mid-systolic murmur at the right and left upper sternal borders (diaphragm, sitting forward) radiating to the right clavicle and both carotids; peak moves later and S2 softens/becomes single (or paradoxically split) with severity; in critical AS with failing LV the murmur gets softer and shorter. Gallavardin: higher-pitched musical component at the apex mimicking MR. Ejection click early after S1 in bicuspid valves, unaffected by manoeuvres. S4 may be audible/palpable. Louder with leg raise, squatting, Valsalva release and after a PVC; softer with Valsalva strain and handgrip. A normally split S2 is the one finding that reliably excludes severe AS. Slow-rising small carotid pulse. ECG: LVH ± strain pattern. — [MSD Manual, Aortic Stenosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/aortic-stenosis)
- Ruling in AS: slow carotid upstroke LR+ 2.8–130, mid-to-late peaking murmur LR+ 8.0–101, soft S2 LR+ 3.1–50; ruling out: no radiation to the right carotid LR− 0.05–0.10. Bedside agreement on presence of a systolic murmur only fair (kappa 0.30–0.48). — [Etchells et al., JAMA 1997 (PMID 9032164)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9032164/)
- Absent S2 LR 12.7, delayed carotid upstroke LR 6.8, humming quality LR 8.5 for aortic valve disease; examination cannot reliably separate severe from less severe AS. — [McGee, Am J Med 2010, doi:10.1016/j.amjmed.2010.04.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2010.04.027)

#### Aortic regurgitation
- Blowing, high-pitched decrescendo diastolic murmur starting just after A2, loudest at the 3rd–4th left parasternal space or apex; diaphragm, leaning forward, breath held in end-expiration; louder with squatting and handgrip. Chronic: S1 normal, S2 loud/sharp and unsplit, wide pulse pressure and peripheral signs; ejection flow murmur and to-and-fro murmur possible. Austin Flint: low-pitched mid-to-late diastolic apical rumble mimicking MS. Acute: S1 absent, prominent S3, murmur short and soft or absent, tachycardia, hypotension. ECG: LVH/repolarisation changes, left atrial enlargement. — [MSD Manual, Aortic Regurgitation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/aortic-regurgitation)
- An early diastolic murmur has LR+ 8.8–32 for mild-or-greater AR and 4.0–8.3 for moderate-or-greater; its absence LR− 0.2–0.3 (mild+) and 0.1 (moderate+); louder with transient arterial occlusion (LR+ 8.4). — [Choudhry & Etchells, JAMA 1999, doi:10.1001/jama.281.23.2231](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.281.23.2231)

#### Mitral stenosis
- Loud S1 at the apex (absent if heavily calcified); opening snap in early diastole, very high-pitched, loudest near the lower LSB, moving closer to S2 as stenosis worsens; low-pitched rumbling decrescendo-crescendo diastolic murmur, bell at the apex in LLD at end-expiration, with presystolic accentuation in sinus rhythm (lost in AF); louder after exercise, squatting, handgrip; accentuated P2 with pulmonary hypertension. ECG: left atrial enlargement (broad notched P, negative terminal P in V1), later RVH/right axis, often AF. — [MSD Manual, Mitral Stenosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/mitral-stenosis); [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- Opening snap vs split S2: snap louder at the apex and on inspiration, A2–OS interval widens on standing. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)

#### Mitral regurgitation
- Holosystolic murmur at the apex (diaphragm, LLD) radiating to the left axilla; may be late-systolic in mild MR; constant or crescendo to S2; posterior-leaflet prolapse gives a coarse murmur radiating to the upper sternum (mimics AS). Louder with handgrip and squatting, softer with standing and Valsalva. S1 soft (occasionally loud); apical S3 and short mid-diastolic flow rumble mark severe MR. Acute MR: signs of acute failure/shock, sinus rhythm. ECG: left atrial enlargement, LVH; AF common in chronic MR. — [MSD Manual, Mitral Regurgitation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/mitral-regurgitation)
- Blowing quality; radiates to axilla and inferior left scapula. — [UW Advanced Physical Diagnosis](https://depts.washington.edu/physdx/heart/tech.html)

#### Mitral valve prolapse
- Crisp mid-systolic click at the apex (diaphragm, LLD) followed by a late-systolic murmur if MR is present. Standing or Valsalva (smaller LV) move the click towards S1 and make the murmur longer/louder; squatting and handgrip delay the click and shorten the murmur. Occasional transient systolic "honk" or "whoop". Ventricular ectopy common. — [MSD Manual, MVP](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/mitral-valve-prolapse-mvp)

#### Tricuspid regurgitation
- Holosystolic murmur at the left mid/lower sternal border or epigastrium, louder on inspiration (Carvallo); high-pitched when secondary to pulmonary hypertension, medium-pitched when severe from other causes; often inaudible, in which case the jugular c-v wave and systolic hepatic pulsation make the diagnosis. RV S3 near the sternum; loud P2 if pulmonary hypertension. ECG usually normal; may show tall peaked P, RVH pattern in V1, AF. — [MSD Manual, Tricuspid Regurgitation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/valvular-disorders/tricuspid-regurgitation)

#### Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy
- Late-peaking systolic murmur at the LSB 3rd–4th ICS; louder with Valsalva and squat-to-stand, softer with handgrip, sustained squatting and passive leg raise; separate apical MR murmur possible; S4 often present; brisk bifid carotid pulse (unlike AS). ECG: LVH with deep T inversion/ST depression, Q waves, left atrial enlargement, sometimes bundle branch block or pre-excitation. — [MSD Manual, HCM](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/cardiomyopathies/hypertrophic-cardiomyopathy)
- Accuracy of those manoeuvres: see Lembo above. — [Lembo et al., NEJM 1988, doi:10.1056/NEJM198806163182404](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM198806163182404)

#### Congenital lesions (brief)
- ASD: wide, fixed splitting of S2; soft grade 2–3/6 mid-systolic murmur at the upper LSB; low-pitched tricuspid diastolic flow rumble with a large shunt; RV heave. ECG: rSR′ in V1, right axis deviation, RVH. — [MSD Manual, ASD](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/congenital-cardiovascular-anomalies/atrial-septal-defect-asd)
- VSD: holosystolic murmur at the lower LSB (louder there than at the apex), small defects grade 1–2 high-pitched short systolic up to 3–4/6 holosystolic with thrill; moderate defects add an apical diastolic flow rumble and accentuated P2; large defects have an attenuated murmur as pressures equalise. ECG normal if small; RVH or biventricular hypertrophy if large. — [MSD Manual, VSD](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/congenital-cardiovascular-anomalies/ventricular-septal-defect-vsd)
- PDA: continuous "machinery" murmur loudest at the 2nd ICS below the medial left clavicle/upper LSB, bounding pulses and wide pulse pressure with a significant shunt; the diastolic component shortens and disappears as pulmonary resistance rises. ECG normal or LVH. — [MSD Manual, PDA](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/congenital-cardiovascular-anomalies/patent-ductus-arteriosus-pda); [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)

#### Pericarditis, tamponade, constriction
- Friction rub: high-pitched, scratchy/creaking ("leather"), classically triphasic (ventricular systole, early diastole, atrial systole) but often intermittent or systolic only; best leaning forward or on hands and knees in held expiration. ECG stage 1: diffuse concave ST elevation with PR depression (reciprocal in aVR), no Q waves. — [MSD Manual, Pericarditis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/pericarditis/pericarditis); [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)
- Effusion/tamponade: muffled heart sounds (unless effusion small), tachycardia, hypotension, distended neck veins, pulsus paradoxus > 10 mm Hg. ECG: low voltage, electrical alternans (neither sensitive nor specific). — [MSD Manual, Pericarditis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/pericarditis/pericarditis)
- In tamponade: pulsus paradoxus pooled sensitivity 82%, tachycardia 77%, raised JVP 76%; pulsus paradoxus > 10 mm Hg LR 3.3, ≤ 10 mm Hg LR 0.03. — [Roy et al., JAMA 2007, doi:10.1001/jama.297.16.1810](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.297.16.1810)
- Constriction: pericardial knock = loud early-diastolic sound (a loud S3 without S4) from abrupt halt of filling. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)

#### Prosthetic valves
- The reference description of normal sounds of ball, disc, porcine and bileaflet valves is Smith, Raizada & Abrams 1981 (abstract only read: mechanisms and timing of normal prosthetic sounds are reviewed). — [Smith et al., Ann Intern Med 1981, doi:10.7326/0003-4819-95-5-594](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-95-5-594)
- Secondary summaries (search-result excerpts, pages not opened): tilting-disc and bileaflet valves give a loud, high-frequency metallic closing click and a softer opening click; caged-ball valves give opening and closing sounds of similar intensity; a muffled or absent click suggests thrombosis/obstruction; a mitral prosthesis should not have a holosystolic murmur nor an aortic prosthesis a sustained diastolic murmur; a short systolic ejection murmur across an aortic prosthesis and a short diastolic murmur across a mitral bioprosthesis can be normal. — [Cleveland Clinic J Med 2002;69:75 (PDF)](https://www.ccjm.org/content/69/1/75.full.pdf); [Relias/Clinician.com, Complications of prosthetic heart valves in the ED](https://www.clinician.com/articles/17474-complications-of-prosthetic-heart-valves-in-the-emergency-department)
- "Prosthetic valve sounds" was one of the 14 key events in the Mayo/Chizner test set. — [March et al., Mayo Clin Proc 2005, doi:10.4065/80.11.1443](https://doi.org/10.4065/80.11.1443)

#### Candidate "core set" used in published tests
- 14 events (March 2005): wide split S2 of RBBB, fixed split + systolic murmur of ASD, click + late systolic murmur of MVP, S4, S3, opening snap of MS, innocent systolic murmur, MR, TR, HOCM, PDA, friction rub, prosthetic valve sounds, alternation in heart failure. — [March et al., Mayo Clin Proc 2005, doi:10.4065/80.11.1443](https://doi.org/10.4065/80.11.1443)
- Repetition-training sets: four basic murmurs (Barrett 2004); AS, AR, MS, MR and innocent systolic murmur (Barrett internist study). — [Barrett et al., Chest 2004, doi:10.1378/chest.126.2.470](https://doi.org/10.1378/chest.126.2.470); [ScienceDaily/Temple release 2007](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/03/070325111532.htm)

### Inferences
- Because S1 intensity is driven by the PR interval and S2 splitting by ventricular activation order, a simulator that already has `TimingEngine` events (atrial activation, ventricular onset per chamber, valve-closure surrogates) can derive rhythm-dependent sounds deterministically: soft S1 at long PR, beat-to-beat S1 variation whenever atria and ventricles are dissociated (complete block, VT, ventricular pacing without AV sync), absent S4 in AF/flutter, wide split with RBBB, reversed split with LBBB/RV pacing, summation gallop above a rate threshold.
- Variable S1 in VT is an inference from the general AV-dissociation statement, not a VT-specific citation. The same reasoning predicts a progressively softer S1 across a Wenckebach cycle and a loud S1 with a short PR (e.g. pre-excitation), which is standard teaching but was not confirmed in a source read for these notes.
- Cycle-length-dependent murmur loudness (AS louder after a PVC or a long RR in AF; MR essentially unchanged) is a cheap, high-value teaching contrast that ties the audio to the ECG strip.
- Manoeuvres can be offered as buttons (inspire, Valsalva, squat, stand, handgrip) changing murmur gain/click timing according to the MSD table; Lembo's sensitivities show the real effects are probabilistic, so the simulator should present them as "typical" responses.
- Several findings are primarily non-auditory (cannon a waves, pulse deficit, pulsus paradoxus, carotid contour). A sound-only feature under-teaches these; a JVP/pulse annotation next to the phonocardiogram would cover them.
- For rhythm-only scenarios (Mobitz II, flutter, PACs) the honest content is "sounds follow the ventricular rhythm; pauses are silent", plus the sourced neck-vein/pulse signs.

### Gaps
- Clinical Methods (NCBI Bookshelf) and StatPearls chapters could not be opened (bot check); Constant's *Bedside Cardiology* and the ACC/AHA valvular guideline text were not consulted. Fine detail such as A2–OS intervals in ms, split widths in ms, and typical grades should be taken from those before parameterising synthesis.
- No source read here describes auscultation specifically in Mobitz I/II, atrial flutter (flutter waves in the JVP, regular vs variable block), PACs, or sinus bradycardia.
- WPW: only a tertiary source (Wikipedia) for paradoxical splitting; no source for loud S1 with short PR.
- LVH/hypertension: "loud A2" not found in a source read; only the S4 mechanism is cited.
- Pulmonary embolism: per-cent prevalence of accentuated P2 (PIOPED data, [Stein et al. 2007, doi:10.1016/j.amjmed.2007.03.024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2007.03.024)) is not in the abstract and the full text could not be retrieved.
- Prosthetic valves: primary descriptions (Smith 1981; [Vongpatanasin et al., NEJM 1996, doi:10.1056/NEJM199608083350607](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199608083350607)) not read in full; the click/murmur rules above rest on search-result excerpts.
- Frequency content: web sources disagree (S3/S4 quoted as 20–200 Hz, 10–50 Hz, or ≤ 20 Hz for S4; S1/S2 20–250 Hz; murmurs up to ~600 Hz) and none was a verified primary measurement; see Part B gaps.
- Prevalence of a physiological S3 in athletes: no figure found.

## Part B — What is the evidence on how to teach cardiac auscultation with recorded or simulated sounds?

### Takeaway
Baseline skill is poor at every level (about 20% of recorded events recognised by residents), and the best-supported remedy is high-volume, focused repetition of a small set of sounds (about 400–500 listens per sound lifted scores from roughly 20–40% to 80–90%), delivered equally well unsupervised. Adding a synchronised visual trace gives a further modest but real gain (d ≈ 0.4–0.45), self-testing with feedback and hands-on practice matter, expensive manikins are not necessary, and headphones are the standard advice; evidence on playback slowing, frequency shifting and speaker limitations is essentially absent.

### Cited Findings

#### How bad is baseline skill?
- 453 residents and 88 students, 12 recorded events: residents recognised 20% on average (median 2.5 of 12 internal medicine, 2.0 family practice), little improvement with year of training, not better than students. — [Mangione & Nieman, JAMA 1997 (PMID 9286830)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286830/)
- A commentary on that study reports cardiologists given the same test scored 80–90% (secondary source, not in the abstract). — [Relias/Clinician.com commentary](https://www.clinician.com/articles/56288-regular-rate-and-rhythm-with-no-murmurs-rubs-or-gallops)
- Only 27.1% of internal medicine and 37.1% of cardiology programmes offered structured auscultation teaching; median accuracy 21.9% for cardiology fellows and 19.3% for medical residents. — [Mangione et al., Ann Intern Med 1993, doi:10.7326/0003-4819-119-1-199307010-00009](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-119-1-199307010-00009)
- 860 participants, 50-question multimedia test: scores did not improve after year 3 of medical school through residency to faculty; only cardiology fellows were better; specificity for systolic murmurs 0.35, sensitivity for diastolic murmurs 0.49. Authors call for training in simultaneous audio and visual examination. — [Vukanovic-Criley et al., Arch Intern Med 2006, doi:10.1001/archinte.166.6.610](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.166.6.610)
- On the Harvey simulator, housestaff diagnosed MR 52%, MS 37%, AR 54%, with no change over a year; recognising a few key observations (murmur contour in MR; opening snap and diastolic rumble in MS) predicted a correct diagnosis. — [St Clair et al., Ann Intern Med 1992, doi:10.7326/0003-4819-117-9-751](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-117-9-751)
- At the bedside, residents heard 40% of extra sounds and made 24% correct diagnoses versus 69%/62% for expert cardiologists. — [Favrat et al., BMC Med Educ 2004, doi:10.1186/1472-6920-4-5](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6920-4-5)
- Students repeatedly struggled to separate regurgitant from ejection murmurs and to characterise S2. — [Finley et al., Med Educ 1998, doi:10.1046/j.1365-2923.1998.00210.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2923.1998.00210.x)

#### Repetition
- 51 second-year students; 500 repetitions of each of four basic murmurs: monitored group 13.5 ± 9.8% → 85 ± 17.6%; unmonitored group 20.9 ± 10.9% → 86.1 ± 15.6%; controls 24 ± 21.7% → 32 ± 22.5% (p < 0.001). Conclusion: auscultation is partly a technical skill. — [Barrett et al., Chest 2004, doi:10.1378/chest.126.2.470](https://doi.org/10.1378/chest.126.2.470)
- Follow-up paper (no abstract available); press coverage reports 80 third-year students, about 500 repetitions each of six abnormal sounds (including AS, MR, S3, S4), proficiency about 39% → 89%, no improvement in controls. — [Barrett et al., Am J Med 2006, doi:10.1016/j.amjmed.2004.12.036](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2004.12.036); figures from [ScienceDaily 2006](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/01/060118082634.htm)
- 149 general internists, one 90-minute session, at least 400 repetitions each of five murmurs (AS, AR, MS, MR, innocent): about 80% correct afterwards versus about 40% in earlier studies (conference report/press release; the 40% is a historical benchmark, not a pre-test). Sounds were distributed on iPods/CD through an ACC partnership. — [ScienceDaily/Temple release 2007](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/03/070325111532.htm)
- The American College of Cardiology lists a "Heart Songs" product built on this approach (page not opened). — [ACC Heart Songs 5](https://www.acc.org/HeartSongs5)
- Three sessions of discussion plus repeated listening to simulated sounds raised family-practice residents from 36% to 62%. — [Horiszny, Fam Med 2001 (PMID 11199907)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11199907/)

#### Visual display with the audio
- RCT, 135 first-year students, 7 training cases with or without a phonocardiogram video (waveform with sweeping cursor), tested on 14 audio-only cases immediately and at 7 days: key-feature score 70% (95% CI 67–75) vs 61% (56–66), p = 0.015, d = 0.45; diagnostic accuracy 68% (62–73) vs 59% (54–65), p = 0.035, d = 0.40. First direct comparison of audio+phonocardiogram against audio alone. — [Watsjold et al., Perspect Med Educ 2021, doi:10.1007/s40037-020-00646-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40037-020-00646-5)
- Design details of that module: self-paced web pages usable on computer, tablet or phone; chest diagram with four listening sites each playing its own recording; learners tick features and pick a diagnosis, with immediate visual feedback; rationale from multimedia-learning principles of contiguity and signalling. — [Watsjold et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40037-020-00646-5)
- Viewing an acoustic cardiogram while listening raised S3 detection accuracy by 8–18% and S4 by 15–32% in experienced listeners (p < 0.05) but only 2% and 11% in first-year students; S4 was harder than S3 for all groups. — [Michaels et al., Clin Cardiol 2010, doi:10.1002/clc.20586](https://doi.org/10.1002/clc.20586)
- Digital-phonocardiography "virtual patient" courses for 106 primary-care physicians: abnormal-murmur detection 73% → 92.5%, extra sounds 17% → 40% (p < 0.001); innocent murmurs still over-called (22% → 25%). — [Germanakis et al., Acta Paediatr 2013, doi:10.1111/apa.12062](https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.12062)
- Electronic stethoscope linked to a laptop visualising murmurs "greatly facilitated teaching" in a preliminary student trial (no numbers). — [Woywodt et al., Postgrad Med J 2004, doi:10.1136/pgmj.2003.014944](https://doi.org/10.1136/pgmj.2003.014944)
- Expert review: digital tools allow replay at full or half speed with no loss of frequency representation, and display as waveform and as spectrogram; the spectral display has advantages and both should be shown together. — [Tavel, Circulation 2006, doi:10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.105.591149](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.105.591149)
- Pilot RCT (n = 32): multisensory module (visual + auditory + tactile) median diagnostic accuracy 100% vs 60% with traditional teaching (p < 0.042). — [Patrizio et al., Int J Med Educ 2024, doi:10.5116/ijme.6607.27a6](https://doi.org/10.5116/ijme.6607.27a6)
- Virtual patient examinations (bedside video with sound) during a clerkship: 58.7 → 73.5 (p = 0.0001) vs controls 60.1 → 59.5; 83.6 at 14 months in 8 retested students. — [Vukanovic-Criley et al., Clin Cardiol 2008, doi:10.1002/clc.20213](https://doi.org/10.1002/clc.20213)

#### Simulators, computer tutorials, quizzes
- Meta-analysis (18 studies): simulation-based training vs no intervention, pooled effect 1.10 (95% CI 0.49–1.72) for knowledge and 0.87 (0.52–1.22) for skills, with high heterogeneity (I² > 90%); hands-on practice appeared important; similar to other active teaching methods in the few comparative studies. — [McKinney et al., J Gen Intern Med 2013, doi:10.1007/s11606-012-2198-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-012-2198-y)
- Mastery learning (computer tutorial + patient simulator, must reach a pass mark): trained third-years 93.8% vs untrained fourth-years 73.9% on simulated sounds, 81.8% vs 75.1% on real patients. — [Butter et al., J Gen Intern Med 2010, doi:10.1007/s11606-010-1309-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-010-1309-x)
- Harvey: 208 fourth-year students at five schools; simulator group scored higher on knowledge, simulator skills and real-patient skills; skills transferred to patients. — [Ewy et al., J Med Educ 1987, doi:10.1097/00001888-198709000-00005](https://doi.org/10.1097/00001888-198709000-00005)
- Deliberate practice with simulation gave large pre–post gains in residents with little faculty time. — [Issenberg et al., Teach Learn Med 2002, doi:10.1207/S15328015TLM1404_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15328015TLM1404_4)
- CD-ROM in quiz format with linked text was equivalent overall to cardiologist-led classroom teaching (classroom better on open questions); students preferred a combination. — [Finley et al., Med Educ 1998, doi:10.1046/j.1365-2923.1998.00210.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2923.1998.00210.x)
- Cognitive load matters: 25–30% of students fail to recognise a murmur they were trained on one hour earlier; higher cognitive load during high-fidelity simulation lowered the odds of recognising the trained murmur (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.11–0.67). — [Fraser et al., Med Educ 2012, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2012.04355.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2012.04355.x)
- Live-patient conference with wireless stethophones: 26.3% → 44.7% on 14 events (p < 0.001), i.e. a smaller gain than the repetition studies. — [March et al., Mayo Clin Proc 2005, doi:10.4065/80.11.1443](https://doi.org/10.4065/80.11.1443)
- 2023 review of 14 studies (2010–2021): 12 reported positive outcomes of simulation; calls for cheaper, standardised approaches. — [Patrizio et al., Cureus 2023, doi:10.7759/cureus.41567](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.41567)
- Web-platform-assisted class scored higher than a traditional class (n = 284, non-randomised, r = 0.246). — [Wang et al., Adv Med Educ Pract 2026, doi:10.2147/AMEP.S630648](https://doi.org/10.2147/AMEP.S630648)

#### Headphones, speakers and low-frequency sounds
- The Watsjold trial told participants to use headphones, mastered its audio on standard earbuds "such that participants would not require high-fidelity equipment", and gated entry with a 200 Hz test-tone volume-discrimination check to allow for hearing, headphone and environment differences. — [Watsjold et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40037-020-00646-5)
- A review of auscultation apps states the sounds are best heard with headphones in a quiet room, though auditorium speakers worked for group teaching. — [ACOFP journal 2013, "Apps improving cardiac auscultation"](https://www.acofp.org/news-and-publications/journal/article-detail/vol-5-no-5-(2013)/apps-improving-cardiac-auscultation)
- An app vendor states that phone speakers roll off the low frequencies carrying S3, S4 and the MS rumble and recommends headphones (vendor claim, not independent evidence). — [Heart Murmurs Pro, App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/id451697322)
- S3, S4 and the MS rumble are low-pitched and need the bell with light pressure even at the bedside. — [MSD Manual, Cardiac Auscultation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/approach-to-the-cardiac-patient/cardiac-auscultation)

### Inferences
- Design the core loop around short, loopable clips of a single finding that the learner can hear hundreds of times; Barrett's results suggest a counter towards roughly 400–500 listens per sound is a meaningful target, and that unsupervised practice works as well as supervised.
- Start with a small set (the four to six classic murmurs plus S3/S4), then widen to the 12–14-event sets used by Mangione and March; add normal-versus-abnormal comparison because learners over-call innocent murmurs and confuse ejection with regurgitant murmurs.
- Show a synchronised phonocardiogram with a moving cursor, aligned to the ECG strip the simulator already draws; the measured gain is modest for novices, larger for gallops in experienced listeners, so the trace should be switchable off for audio-only self-testing (the trials tested audio-only).
- Add a quiz mode with immediate feature-level feedback (tick features, then diagnosis) and a pass mark, mirroring the mastery-learning and Watsjold designs; emphasise a few key discriminating observations per lesion (St Clair).
- Keep the listening screen simple: the cognitive-load finding argues against burying the sound in a busy scenario while the learner is first acquiring it.
- Audio output: advise headphones on first use and offer a low-frequency test tone/check, as Watsjold did. Because small speakers reproduce little below roughly 100–200 Hz (general acoustics, not a cited study), a "small speaker" mode that boosts or transposes gallops upward is a reasonable engineering option, but it has no educational evidence behind it and should be labelled as altered.
- Half-speed playback with pitch preserved is recommended by an expert (Tavel) and is cheap to offer; treat it as an aid without proven effect.
- Anatomical listening sites with site-specific recordings (as in Watsjold) fit naturally onto the existing 3-D torso.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found on slowed playback, on frequency shifting/transposition of S3/S4, on headphones versus laptop/phone speakers, or on measured speaker roll-off for heart sounds; the only material is vendor advice, an app review and expert opinion.
- No verified primary source for the frequency bands of S3/S4 (web figures range from ≤ 20 Hz to 20–200 Hz); a signal-processing reference should be consulted before designing filters.
- The Barrett 2006 Am J Med paper has no abstract and the internist study was found only as a press release; exact n, repetition counts and statistics need the full papers. No dose-response study (e.g. 100 vs 500 repetitions) and no long-term retention data for repetition training were found.
- Watsjold's 7-day retention results are not broken out in the abstract; the full text opened only in part.
- No formal position statement on auscultation teaching from the ACC, AHA or ESC was located; only the ACC's distribution of the repetition programme.
- The McKinney meta-analysis subgroup details (feedback, time on task, fidelity) were not read beyond the abstract; a later review reportedly finding no difference between high- and low-fidelity simulation (Osborne/Mostafa) was seen only in a search summary and could not be verified.
- No study was found on mixed/interleaved practice or contrasting cases specifically for heart sounds, nor a randomised trial of a smartphone auscultation app.
- Most training studies test recognition of recorded sounds shortly after training; transfer to real patients is shown only by Ewy 1987 and Butter 2010.
